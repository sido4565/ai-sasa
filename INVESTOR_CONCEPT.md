# AI Sasa — Investor Concept (working name)

**Status:** Initial concept, 26 October 2026. Name and availability are unverified.

## The company

AI Sasa helps Kenyan organisations understand, adopt and safely use AI in everyday work. It combines practical training, workflow implementation and ongoing measurement, with materials adapted to Kenyan business contexts, local data-protection requirements and different levels of digital access.

**Positioning:** From AI awareness to measurable productivity.

**Mission:** Make useful, responsible AI accessible to Kenyan workers and organisations.

## The problem

Many organisations are curious about AI, but lack the skills, trusted guidance and hands-on support to turn experimentation into reliable business results. Generic online courses do not tailor tools to local workflows, and a one-off workshop rarely changes how teams work.

A 2025 survey of 600+ MSMEs across Kenya, Ethiopia, Nigeria and South Africa reported that 53% had adopted AI tools in some form. This is an early signal, not a Kenya-only or nationally representative estimate. Kenya's 2025–2030 AI Strategy and implementation roadmap also call for workforce training and broader digital literacy.

## Initial customer and wedge

Start with **Kenyan SMEs with 10–250 staff**, initially in Nairobi and nearby counties, where a founder or operations lead can approve a pilot quickly. Prioritise customer-facing and administrative workflows that are repetitive and low-risk:

- Customer support and sales follow-up
- Marketing and content production
- Document handling, reporting and internal knowledge search
- Finance and operations support, with human review

Secondary buyers after repeatable SME pilots: banks, insurers, telcos, universities, TVETs, county programmes and development organisations.

## Product and delivery

1. **AI Readiness Sprint:** workflow discovery, staff baseline, risk and data review, prioritised use cases.
2. **Role-based training:** hands-on sessions for managers and frontline teams, including prompting, verification, privacy, bias, security and policy.
3. **Implementation:** configure existing AI tools and lightweight automations around approved workflows; human approval for consequential outputs.
4. **AI adoption subscription:** office hours, onboarding, policy updates, workflow support and quarterly outcome reporting.
5. **AI Sasa Academy:** repeatable short courses and train-the-trainer materials for wider reach, including low-bandwidth formats and Swahili/local-language modules where validated with users.

## Business model

- Fixed-fee readiness and training engagements
- Implementation fees for workflow projects
- Monthly/annual subscriptions for continuing support and measurement
- Sponsored cohort delivery through employers, education partners and development programmes
- Later: licensed curriculum, trainer certification and a lightweight adoption dashboard

Keep the first product service-led to learn what customers will pay for. Build software only after repeated workflows and retention needs are demonstrated.

## Why this can win

- Contextual delivery grounded in Kenyan workflows, data practices and regulation
- An end-to-end offer: awareness → use-case selection → implementation → measured outcomes
- A reusable curriculum, workflow library and evaluation framework across sectors
- Local trainer and partner network capable of serving organisations beyond Nairobi
- Trust built through privacy-conscious practices and clear human accountability

The defensibility must be earned through customer results, repeatable delivery, trusted distribution and accumulated implementation know-how; training content alone is easy to copy.

## Pilot and evidence plan (first 90 days)

Recruit 5–10 SMEs for paid or co-funded pilots. Select one or two workflows per organisation and record a baseline before training. At 30 and 60 days, measure active use, staff confidence, time per task, error/rework rates and customer response times where relevant. Document tool costs and safeguards. Seek permission before using customer logos, quotes or case studies.

**Investor proof points to target:** paid pilot conversion, repeat/renewal demand, gross margin by delivery format, staff activation after 30 days, verified workflow outcomes, referrals and delivery cost per organisation. These are targets to test, not current results.

## Go-to-market

- Founder-led outreach to SME associations, professional bodies, coworking spaces, banks and accounting/HR service providers
- Free monthly practical clinics as a lead channel, converting interested organisations into paid readiness sprints
- Partner cohorts with TVETs, universities, county business programmes and development organisations
- Publish short Kenyan case studies showing measured before/after workflow results
- Recruit and certify a facilitator network only after the core programme is validated

## Fundraising logic

Avoid pitching a large software platform before product-market evidence. First fund a 6–12 month validation phase: delivery team, curriculum, pilot support, measurement, partner acquisition and responsible data practices. Set the raise amount only after costing the pilot plan and confirming founder contribution, customer revenue and grant/partner options. For investors, present a milestone-based use of funds and a credible path from services to repeatable, higher-margin programmes and software.

## Key risks and responses

- **Crowded training market:** sell implementation and measurable adoption, not certificates alone.
- **Low ability to pay among microbusinesses:** begin with SMEs and sponsored cohorts; validate willingness to pay before scaling.
- **Tool/vendor changes:** teach transferable skills and support multiple providers.
- **Privacy, accuracy and bias:** use data minimisation, approved tools, human review and clear escalation rules.
- **Weak impact claims:** baseline every pilot and report only observed outcomes.
- **Founder-dependent delivery:** standardise playbooks, trainer onboarding and quality checks after pilots.

## What we need to decide next

1. Founder strengths and existing access to customers or training partners
2. First customer segment: SMEs, schools/TVETs, or corporate teams
3. City/region and delivery languages
4. Whether to begin as a trading company, a social enterprise, or pair a commercial company with a nonprofit training arm
5. Available startup capital and the first pilot budget
